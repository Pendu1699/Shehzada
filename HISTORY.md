# History notes

This file gives a short note and source for every scripted event and figure, marks
where a date or detail is uncertain, and lists the game's deliberate simplifications.
It is written for players, teachers and reviewers who want to know what is history
and what is game.

**General sources** (standard works; used throughout):

- Hari Ram Gupta, *History of the Sikhs*, vols. II–V (Munshiram Manoharlal).
- J. S. Grewal, *The Sikhs of the Punjab* (New Cambridge History of India, 1990; rev. 1998).
- Khushwant Singh, *A History of the Sikhs*, vol. 1 (1963; OUP 2004).
- Bhagat Singh, *Sikh Polity in the Eighteenth and Nineteenth Centuries* (1978).
- Ganda Singh, *Ahmad Shah Durrani* (1959); *The Punjab in 1839–40* (1952).
- Harbans Singh (ed.), *The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism*, Punjabi University, Patiala.
- J. D. Cunningham, *A History of the Sikhs* (1849).
- Fauja Singh, *Military System of the Sikhs* (1964).

Where these disagree on a date, the game follows Gupta or Grewal and says so below.

---

## Calendar and turns

- Each turn is half a year, opening on **Vaisakhi** (spring) or **Diwali** (autumn), the two
  occasions on which the Sarbat Khalsa traditionally met at Amritsar in the misl era
  (Bhagat Singh, ch. 5). The monsoon falls in the Vaisakhi→Diwali half; Afghan invasions
  came in the cold season.
- An event that happened between two festivals is placed on the turn that covers it: e.g.
  the Vadda Ghallughara (5 February 1762) falls on the **Diwali 1761** turn.

## The wider subcontinent

The map runs from Herat and Balkh to Dhaka and Chittagong, from Ladakh and Ngari to Lhasa, and
down to Travancore and Ceylon. Beyond the Punjab, frontiers follow a timeline in
`src/engine/history.ts` rather than AI conquest, so the century unfolds roughly as it did unless
the player holds the land. Key dates used:

- **1751** Balkh to the Durrani; Orissa ceded to the Bhonsles. **1752** Khandesh to the Peshwa.
- **1756–1760** Ajmer, Ahmedabad and Bijapur to the Marathas; Surat castle and Chittagong to the
  Company.
- **1757** Plassey. **1761** Panipat; the Jats take Agra; Hyder Ali takes power in Mysore.
- **1764** Buxar. **1765** the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa granted to the Company.
- **1768** Prithvi Narayan Shah takes Kathmandu. **1770** the Bengal famine.
- **1774** Awadh conquers Rohilkhand. **1775** Benares to the Company.
- **1783–84** the Chalisa famine. **1784–85** Mahadji Scindia takes Gwalior and Agra.
- **1790** Gorkhas take Kumaon (Garhwal 1804). **1792** Malabar to the Company.
- **1799** Srirangapatna; Tipu Sultan killed. **1801** Awadh's cessions; the Carnatic annexed.
- **1803** Assaye and Laswari; Agra and Orissa to the Company. **1805** Holkar in the Punjab.
- **1815** Treaty of Sugauli (Kumaon and Garhwal); the British take Kandy.
- **1818** the end of the Peshwa's rule; the Rajput and Maratha princes accept paramountcy.
- **1826** Bharatpur stormed. **1834** Zorawar Singh conquers Ladakh; **1840** Baltistan;
  **1841** the Tibet campaign and his death at To-yo. **1842** the retreat from Kabul.
- **1843** Sindh annexed after Miani and Dubba. **1844** Gwalior after Maharajpur.

Princely states under the Company's paramountcy after 1818 are drawn as Company land, which is a
simplification: they kept their own rulers.

## Alternative history

The game's forks (`src/data/events/alt.ts`) are labelled in play as roads history did not take:
an early Sarkar-i-Khalsa under another misl (Jassa Singh Ahluwalia was hailed Sultan-ul-Qaum and
the Sikhs struck coin at Lahore in 1765, but no state was made until Ranjit Singh); a permanent
confederacy of the misls; Ranjit Singh entering another house's service; and cities the Khalsa
never held for long (Delhi, which Baghel Singh entered in 1783) or never held at all.

## Soldiers on the map

Each army is dressed for who it is (`src/map/soldiers.ts`), after Company-period and Pahari
pictures of soldiers. Sikh misls wear their misl's dastaar and a chola; the Akali-Nihangs of the
Shaheedan and the Budha Dal the blue dumalla with farla and chakars. Afghan forces wear a turban
wound about a peaked kulah, a long chapan and the lungi sash, and carry the jezail. The Mughal
governors and the nawabs wear the flat atpati pagri and the flared jama with a cummerbund. Marathas
wear the rounded pagadi and the white angarkha and carry the swallow-tailed bhagwa jhenda. Company
sepoys wear red coats with white cross-belts (the "Lal Paltan") and the pointed "sundial" turban;
European regulars the shako. Muslim soldiers are drawn with full beards and trimmed upper lips,
Sikhs with full beards and moustaches, Marathas and Gorkhas with moustaches. These are emblems at
map scale: dress varied far more between regiments, decades and individuals than any token can
show. Useful references: the National Army Museum's pages on the armies of the East India Company
and Indian armies in art; the Royal Society's Afghan portraits after James Rattray (1840s); and
museum collections of Mughal and Peshwa-era painting.

## Deliberate simplifications

- **Map.** Sixty-one regions stand in for hundreds of parganas of the Punjab, and eighty-four
  much larger regions for the rest of the subcontinent. Borders are generated from river courses,
  a traced coastline and town positions, not from any historical administrative map.
- **Populations beyond the Punjab** are scaled game figures, not census estimates, and the
  economy counts most of a computer-run power's distant revenue as spent at home.
- **Starting positions.** Ownership in each scenario approximates the most important holdings
  of each power. Misl territories in 1748 are home grounds (deras), not formal states.
- **Population and faith.** Region populations and faith shares are game estimates for c. 1750,
  following the broad picture in Grewal and the 1855/1868 census patterns projected back.
  They should not be quoted as data.
- **Economy.** Revenue, rakhi (about a fifth of produce) and jagir mechanics are modelled on
  the descriptions in Bhagat Singh and Grewal, with game numbers.
- **Unit statistics** are game abstractions.
- **Josh.** The answer of the whole Panth to desecration is history (1740, 1757, 1762, 1764);
  turning it into a timed bonus is a game device.
- **Phulkian misl.** The Phulkian houses were not among the eleven misls of the Dal Khalsa; the
  game treats them as a playable Sikh polity with a distinct path.
- **The Shaheedan and Nishanwalia misls'** territories are approximate.
- **Hill states** are grouped into one AI faction ("Rajput Hill States") except Kangra and Jammu.
- **The Company** is a single faction; its Cis-Sutlej protectorates are represented by alliance
  flags on the protected chiefs.

## Respect

- The Gurus are never depicted; they are referred to with "Ji".
- No Gurbani is quoted, paraphrased or invented. The dhadi's text is original prose.
- No Sikh faction has any option to desecrate; this is enforced in code and tested.
- The game is called *Shehzada* ("prince", the young heir of a house: the sardar you play
  inherits a misl and a century). The phrase "Raj karega Khalsa", which the game uses as a
  historical saying, is not quoted further: the rest of the dohra in which it appears is left out.
